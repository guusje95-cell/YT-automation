# The First Photos of Black Holes, Explained

Video id: `black-hole-photos` · narration 6.1 min · 912 words

## [0:00] Intro

In April 2019, astronomers released a blurry orange ring on a black background, and called it the first image of a black hole. It sits at the centre of a galaxy called M87, about 55 million light-years away, and it weighs about 6.5 billion times as much as the Sun. Three years later, they did it again, this time for the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy. But there is an obvious problem. A black hole traps light. So what exactly is in these pictures, how do you build a telescope sharp enough to take them, and why was the black hole next door the harder one?

- First image released 10 April 2019; M87* ~55 million light-years; ~6.5 billion solar masses — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Sgr A* image released 12 May 2022 (three years later) — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)

## [0:41] 1. What's in the picture

Start with what the image is not. It is not a photograph of the black hole itself. Nothing comes back from inside the event horizon. What you are seeing is the light around it: hot gas swirling near the black hole, glowing in radio waves, with its light bent by the black hole's gravity into a ring. The dark patch in the middle is called the shadow. It is the region where light that would have reached us has been bent away or captured. The shadow is the closest an image can get to the black hole itself. And the event horizon is smaller still. For M87, it is about two and a half times smaller than the shadow, but still just under 40 billion kilometres across.

**Claims → sources**
- Ring-like structure with dark centre: the shadow, caused by light bent and captured near the event horizon — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Event horizon ~2.5 times smaller than the shadow; just under 40 billion km across — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)

## [1:27] A hundred years of bending light

The idea that gravity bends light is older than any radio telescope. Einstein's general relativity predicted it, and in 1919, expeditions to a total solar eclipse tested the theory by checking whether starlight passing close to the Sun was bent. It was, as predicted. A black hole is that same effect taken to the extreme. Near the event horizon, gravity bends light so strongly that it can wrap around the black hole, which is why the glowing gas appears as a ring rather than a smudge. One of the project's lead scientists called the shadow something predicted by Einstein's general relativity that we had never seen before. A century after starlight was first seen bending around the Sun, we could finally see the edge of the place where light cannot escape.

**Claims → sources**
- 1919 eclipse expeditions tested general relativity by checking starlight bending around the Sun, as predicted by Einstein — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Shadow is 'something predicted by Einstein's general relativity that we've never seen before' (Falcke) — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Light bent by gravity near the event horizon; shadow caused by bending and capture — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)

## [2:21] 2. A telescope the size of Earth

The second question is how you take a picture of something so far away. The ring is tiny on the sky. And the sharpness of any telescope depends on how wide it is compared with the wavelength it observes. For radio waves, that means a dish far bigger than anything we could build. To resolve this ring, you would need a telescope roughly the size of the Earth. So astronomers built one, virtually. The Event Horizon Telescope linked eight radio observatories around the world, from Chile to Hawaii to the South Pole, and observed together in 2017 at a wavelength of 1.3 millimetres. By recording the same signals at precisely timed moments and combining them later, the network behaves like a single dish as wide as the planet. The sharpness is hard to picture. ESO compares it to reading a newspaper in New York from a café in Paris. The data volumes were just as extreme. Each telescope produced about 350 terabytes a day, and supercomputers in Germany and the United States combined it into one image. More than 200 researchers worked on that first result. And it was not a one-off. The same ring showed up across multiple independent observations, which is part of why the team was confident it was real and not an artefact of the processing.

**Claims → sources**
- EHT: eight radio telescopes linked into an Earth-sized virtual telescope; 2017 campaign; 1.3 mm wavelength — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Telescopes include ALMA/APEX (Chile), JCMT/SMA (Hawaii), South Pole Telescope — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Resolution 20 micro-arcseconds: 'enough to read a newspaper in New York from a café in Paris' — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- ~350 terabytes per telescope per day; combined at MPIfR (Germany) and MIT Haystack (US) — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)

## [3:52] 3. Why our own black hole was harder

Now the black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, called Sagittarius A star. It is only about 27,000 light-years away, and about four million times the mass of the Sun. Closer should mean easier. It wasn't. Sagittarius A star is more than a thousand times smaller and less massive than the one in M87. Around the giant in M87, gas takes days or weeks to complete an orbit. Around our much smaller black hole, it completes an orbit in minutes. So the source kept changing while the telescopes were watching, a bit like trying to photograph a puppy chasing its tail. The team had to analyse many images and average them to reveal the underlying structure. The result, published in May 2022 by more than 300 researchers, looks strikingly like the first one. Seen from Earth, it is about the same size on the sky as a doughnut sitting on the Moon.

**Claims → sources**
- Sgr A* ~27,000 light-years; ~4 million solar masses — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)
- More than a thousand times smaller and less massive than M87* — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)
- Gas orbits M87* in days to weeks; Sgr A* in minutes; images averaged — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)
- Released 12 May 2022; more than 300 researchers from 80 institutes — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)

## [4:54] What the images tell us

So why does a blurry ring matter so much? Because the size of the shadow depends on the black hole's mass. The more massive the black hole, the larger the shadow. For M87, that let the team measure its enormous mass directly from the image. And for our own black hole, the ring came out almost exactly the size Einstein's general relativity predicted. One of the researchers put it simply: general relativity governs these objects up close. Two black holes, a thousand times apart in mass, look remarkably similar near their edges. Whatever differences we see further out come from the material swirling around them, not from the black holes themselves.

**Claims → sources**
- The more massive a black hole, the larger the shadow; the shadow allowed measuring M87*'s mass — [eso1907](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/)
- Ring size agreed with general relativity predictions; 'General Relativity governs these objects up close' (Markoff) — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)
- The two look remarkably similar near the edge; differences further out due to surrounding material — [eso2208](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/)

## [5:41] Closing

In a few years, astronomers went from drawing black holes to photographing them. Not the black hole itself, which no light can leave, but the ring of light at its edge and the shadow it casts.
