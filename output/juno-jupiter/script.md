# What NASA's Juno Found Inside Jupiter

Video id: `juno-jupiter` · narration 9.0 min · 1255 words

## [0:00] Intro

For most of the last century, the inside of Jupiter was educated guesswork. The diagrams looked tidy: a thick layer of clouds, a deep ocean of hydrogen, and at the very centre, a compact ball of rock and ice. Then, in 2016, a spacecraft arrived that could actually measure the planet, skimming a few thousand kilometres above the cloud tops on every close pass. What it found was messier, deeper, and much stranger than the diagrams. These are five discoveries from NASA's Juno mission, starting at the poles, where no spacecraft had ever had a good look, and ending at a core that appears to be partly dissolved.

## [0:43] The spacecraft

Juno launched from Cape Canaveral on the fifth of August, 2011, and spent five years crossing the solar system. On the fourth of July, 2016, it fired its main engine and slipped into orbit around Jupiter. Two design choices made it unusual. First, it runs on sunlight. Out at Jupiter, sunlight is only about four percent as strong as it is at Earth, so Juno carries three huge solar arrays, each about nine metres long. Second, Jupiter surrounds itself with some of the most intense radiation in the solar system. So Juno's most sensitive electronics sit inside a titanium vault that weighs about 180 kilograms. Instead of circling the equator, Juno loops over the poles on long, stretched orbits. Each close pass is brief. Then the spacecraft swings far back out, and waits weeks for the next one. At first, each of those orbits took 53 days.

## [1:48] 1. Storm geometry at the poles

Discovery number one is at the poles, and it looks almost designed. Over Jupiter's north pole, Juno's infrared camera found one cyclone sitting at the centre, surrounded by a ring of eight more, arranged roughly like an octagon. Over the south pole: one central cyclone, surrounded by five, in a rough pentagon. Each of these storms is thousands of kilometres across, and they are packed so tightly that their edges almost touch. On Earth, hurricanes drift toward the poles and fall apart. On Jupiter, these storms stay put. Scientists watched the same pattern over years of passes. The cyclones wobble around their positions, but they do not merge. Then, in November 2019, a new and smaller cyclone squeezed into the southern group. Nobody fully understands yet how such a pattern forms, or why it stays so stable. That is still an open question.

**Claims → sources**
- North: 8 cyclones around a central one (octagon); south: 5 around central (pentagon) — [adriani_2018](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/83934), [jpl_3d_2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-science-results-offer-first-3d-view-of-jupiter-atmosphere/)
- Cyclones remain in place and oscillate around an equilibrium position — [jpl_3d_2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-science-results-offer-first-3d-view-of-jupiter-atmosphere/)
- New smaller cyclone imaged at south pole on Nov 4, 2019 — [pia23558](https://photojournal.jpl.nasa.gov/catalog/PIA23558)
- How the cyclones persist without merging is unknown — [adriani_2018](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/83934)

## [2:48] 2. Weather that goes deep

The second discovery: Jupiter's weather does not stop at the clouds. The famous stripes are alternating bands of wind, called belts and zones, blowing east and west at hundreds of kilometres per hour. For decades, nobody knew how deep they went. They could have been a thin skin, like weather on Earth. Juno answered that with gravity. As the spacecraft swoops past, tiny tugs on its radio signal reveal how mass is moving inside the planet. The answer: the jet streams reach about 3,000 kilometres down. Then there is the Great Red Spot, a storm wider than our entire planet. Juno's microwave instrument showed its roots extend more than 350 kilometres below the cloud tops. Gravity measurements from two close flybys put the depth at around 500 kilometres. That is deep compared to any storm on Earth, but shallow compared to the jets around it. Imagine a spinning coin, wide and thin, floating on top of winds that go far deeper.

**Claims → sources**
- Belts and zones are alternating jet streams — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)
- Jets extend about 3,000 km below cloud tops — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/), [jpl_3d_2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-science-results-offer-first-3d-view-of-jupiter-atmosphere/)
- Great Red Spot wider than Earth (illustration PIA24817 depicts size) — [jpl_3d_2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-science-results-offer-first-3d-view-of-jupiter-atmosphere/)
- MWR: storms incl. GRS extend >350 km; gravity: GRS ~500 km — [jpl_3d_2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-science-results-offer-first-3d-view-of-jupiter-atmosphere/)

## [3:55] 3. Water, lightning and mushballs

Number three involves something surprisingly basic: water. In 1995, NASA's Galileo probe plunged into Jupiter's atmosphere and measured far less water than expected. It may simply have dropped into an unusually dry spot, a bit like sampling Earth's weather only over a desert. Juno measured from orbit instead, using microwaves to see deeper into the atmosphere, near the equator. There, water makes up about 0.25 percent of the molecules, almost three times the Sun's proportion of oxygen and hydrogen. Then came the lightning. Juno saw small flashes high up in the atmosphere, in places where it should be too cold for liquid water. The proposed explanation is wonderfully odd. Storms may throw ice crystals so high that ammonia gas melts them into a slushy mix of ammonia and water. Those slushy hailstones, nicknamed mushballs, may grow heavy and fall deep into the planet, carrying ammonia and water down with them. It is a model, not a direct observation. But it explains several puzzling measurements at once.

**Claims → sources**
- Galileo probe (1995) found less water than expected; possibly a dry spot — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/), [jpl_water_2020](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/findings-from-nasas-juno-update-jupiter-water-mystery/)
- At equator water ~0.25% of molecules, almost 3x Sun — [jpl_water_2020](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/findings-from-nasas-juno-update-jupiter-water-mystery/)
- Shallow lightning observed at high altitudes — [jpl_mushballs_2020](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/shallow-lightning-and-mushballs-reveal-ammonia-to-nasas-juno-scientists/)
- Mushballs: ammonia-water hail model — [jpl_mushballs_2020](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/shallow-lightning-and-mushballs-reveal-ammonia-to-nasas-juno-scientists/)

## [5:10] 4. A fuzzy core

The fourth discovery goes all the way to the centre. By tracking Juno's radio signal on every close pass, scientists built up a detailed map of Jupiter's gravity. And gravity is shaped by where the mass sits inside. The expected picture was a small, dense core with a sharp boundary. Instead, the measurements point to a core that is larger and partly dissolved, with heavy elements mixed out into the hydrogen around it. Scientists call it a dilute, or fuzzy, core. How did it get that way? One idea, published in Nature in 2019, is a head-on collision early in Jupiter's history, with a young planet around ten times the mass of Earth, violent enough to shatter the original core and stir it outward. Other researchers think the core could have formed this way gradually, or been slowly eroded from within. The question is still open. What changed is that we now have measurements to test the ideas against.

**Claims → sources**
- Gravity science via radio tracking — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)
- Core larger than expected, partially dissolved: dilute/fuzzy — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)
- Giant-impact hypothesis (Liu et al. 2019), impactor ~10 Earth masses — [liu_2019](https://cris.tau.ac.il/en/publications/the-formation-of-jupiters-diluted-core-by-a-giant-impact/)

## [6:14] 5. A lopsided magnetic field

And the fifth discovery is invisible, but enormous. Jupiter's magnetic field is immense, generated by a deep layer of hydrogen squeezed so hard it behaves like a liquid metal. Juno found that the field is stronger and stranger than expected. In some places, it is more than thirty times as strong as Earth's. And it is lopsided. The northern hemisphere is a tangle of intense patches, while near the equator sits an isolated hot spot of magnetic field that scientists call the Great Blue Spot. By comparing Juno's map with measurements from earlier missions, researchers found that the field is slowly changing over time. Most of that change happens at the Great Blue Spot, probably because deep winds are shearing the field and dragging it around. The same magnetic field powers Jupiter's auroras, where Juno measured particle energies of up to 400,000 electron volts, far beyond anything in Earth's northern lights.

**Claims → sources**
- Field generated by liquid metallic hydrogen — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)
- Field >30x Earth's in some places — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)
- Great Blue Spot; secular variation concentrated there; driven by deep winds — [jpl_magfield_2019](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-juno-finds-changes-in-jupiters-magnetic-field/)
- Auroral energies up to 400,000 eV — [nasa_juno](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/juno/)

## [7:19] Bonus: the moons

Juno was built to study Jupiter itself, but in its later years it turned toward the moons. In June 2021, it passed within about 1,000 kilometres of Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system. In September 2022, it flew about 350 kilometres above the icy surface of Europa, a moon thought to hide a salty ocean beneath its shell. Then, in December 2023 and February 2024, it came within about 1,500 kilometres of Io, the most volcanic world we know. Among the images was Loki Patera, a lava lake roughly 200 kilometres across, with islands in the middle and a surface so smooth in places that it reflected sunlight like glass. Each of those flybys also bent Juno's path, shrinking its orbit from 53 days down to 33.

- Ganymede flyby June 2021 within ~1,038 km (narrated as about 1,000 km) — [pia24681](https://photojournal.jpl.nasa.gov/catalog/PIA24681)
- Europa flyby Sept 29, 2022 at ~352 km (narrated as about 350 km) — [pia25330](https://photojournal.jpl.nasa.gov/catalog/PIA25330)
- Io flybys Dec 2023 & Feb 2024 within ~1,500 km — [nasa_io_2024](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/juno/nasas-juno-gives-aerial-views-of-mountain-lava-lake-on-io/)

## [8:21] What it adds up to

Put it all together and Jupiter looks less like a neat diagram and more like a living system. Storms locked into geometric patterns at the poles. Winds reaching thousands of kilometres down. Hail made of ammonia. A magnetic field that drifts over the years. And a core that never quite settled.
