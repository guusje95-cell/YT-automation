# Falling Into Jupiter — narration draft v1

## COLD OPEN
On the seventh of December, 1995, at four minutes past ten at night, Universal Time,
a small probe hit the top of Jupiter's atmosphere at a hundred and seventy thousand kilometres an hour.
Within a minute, the gas in front of it was hotter than the surface of the Sun.
It carried six instruments, a parachute and a radio. It was never meant to come back.
For fifty-eight minutes, it sent data home. Then the signal stopped.
This is the story of how far it got. And of what lies beneath it, all the way to the centre,
in a place no machine will ever reach.

## ARRIVAL
Jupiter is eleven times wider than Earth. More than a thousand Earths could fit inside it.
It holds more than twice the material of every other planet, moon and asteroid in the solar system combined.
And it has no surface. Nothing to land on. Just gas, getting thicker and hotter the deeper you go.
The probe's journey began five months before it arrived. In July 1995, it separated from its mothership,
the Galileo orbiter, about eighty million kilometres from Jupiter, and coasted the rest of the way alone.
Its first danger wasn't the planet itself. About fifty thousand kilometres above the clouds,
it flew through an intense belt of radiation, particles trapped and accelerated by Jupiter's magnetic field.
Hold on to that. We'll find out where that field comes from at the bottom of this journey.

## ENTRY
Jupiter's gravity is so strong that anything falling in from far away hits the atmosphere at close to sixty kilometres per second.
The probe arrived at about forty-seven.
A minute after entry, it was slowing down so hard that it felt two hundred and twenty-eight times the force of gravity.
If you'd been on board, your body would have weighed more than fifteen tonnes.
Its heat shield was taking the hit from gas at sixteen thousand degrees.
Then, as it slowed, a parachute opened, and it began to sink, measuring everything around it.

## THE CLOUDS
At the very top of the atmosphere, the pressure is only a tenth of what you feel at sea level, and the temperature is minus one hundred and sixty degrees.
A little lower, at the same pressure as the air around you right now, it's still minus one hundred and eight.
Jupiter's weather happens in three layers of cloud, about seventy-one kilometres thick in total.
The top layer is probably ammonia ice, the bright white bands you see from space.
Deeper down are clouds of water, and they make lightning.
Juno would later find something stranger in those storms. Lightning flashing high up, where it should be too cold for liquid water.
The likely explanation: hailstones made of ammonia and water slush, nicknamed mushballs, falling out of the storms like slush bombs.
The Galileo probe saw less of all this than anyone expected. Less water, less lightning, and only half the helium scientists had predicted near the top.
Years later, Juno would explain why. The probe had almost certainly fallen into an unusually dry spot, one of the desert patches of Jupiter's sky.

## SIGNAL LOST
Fifty-eight minutes in, the science data stopped.
At sixty-one minutes, the transmitter failed, about a hundred and eighty kilometres below the point where it entered,
at nearly twenty-three times the pressure at sea level.
Whatever was left of it kept sinking into the dark.
From here on down, no machine has ever gone.

## JUNO TAKES OVER
To follow it further, we need a different kind of explorer.
In 2016, NASA's Juno spacecraft began looping over Jupiter's poles, measuring the planet from the outside:
its gravity, its magnetism, and microwaves glowing up from deep below the clouds.
And it found that the weather doesn't stop at the clouds.
The famous stripes are jet streams, and they reach about three thousand kilometres down.
The Great Red Spot, a storm twice as wide as Earth, has roots between three hundred and fifty and five hundred kilometres deep.
A storm wider than our planet, with roots so deep that the probe's entire descent would fit inside them twice.

## THE OCEAN WITH NO SURFACE
Keep falling, and the pressure keeps rising.
Hydrogen, the gas that makes up almost ninety percent of Jupiter, is squeezed so hard that it turns into a liquid.
There is no clear surface to splash into. The gas just gets denser and hotter until, at some point, you're no longer falling through air. You're sinking through an ocean.
And perhaps halfway to the centre, something even stranger happens.
The pressure becomes so great that electrons are squeezed off the hydrogen atoms.
The liquid starts to conduct electricity, like a metal.

## THE ENGINE
This is the answer to the danger at the start of our journey.
An ocean of liquid metal, churning inside a planet that spins once every ten hours, works like a giant dynamo.
It generates a magnetic field sixteen to fifty-four times as powerful as Earth's.
That's the field that trapped the radiation the probe flew through on its way in.
Juno mapped it, and it's lumpy. Near the equator sits an intense patch scientists call the Great Blue Spot,
and the field there is slowly drifting, dragged around by the planet's deep winds.

## THE CORE
And at the very centre? For decades, the diagrams showed a compact ball of rock and ice.
Juno's gravity measurements found something else.
The core looks much larger than expected, and not solid. Partly dissolved, its heavy elements smeared out into the metallic hydrogen around it.
In some models, that fuzzy core stretches more than halfway from the centre to the cloud tops.
How does a planet end up with a core like that? One idea is a collision.
Early in Jupiter's history, a young planet about ten times the mass of Earth may have hit it head-on, hard enough to shatter the original core and stir it outward.
It's a hypothesis. Nobody knows yet. But if it's right, the heart of the biggest planet in the solar system still carries the scar of a crash billions of years old.

## ENDING
Back at the top of the clouds, those fifty-eight minutes of data still matter.
Everything below that point, we know only from measurements taken from far outside.
Jupiter isn't a ball of gas with a rock in the middle.
It's a planet with storms rooted hundreds of kilometres deep, an ocean of liquid metal, and a core that never settled.
And our first real look inside it came from a little machine that fell in, and never came back.
